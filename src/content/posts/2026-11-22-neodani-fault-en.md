In the video I asked one question: can you still see ground that an earthquake lifted more than a century ago? You can, though it is far less obvious than I expected.

Here I want to cover what the video had no room for. If you'd like to see this fault for yourself, how do you get to Midori, where in the museum should you spend your time, and what does "6 metres up and 4 metres sideways" actually mean?

## The morning of 28 October 1891

The Nōbi earthquake struck at 6:38 in the morning on 28 October 1891, with a magnitude of 8.0. It was a shallow, very large earthquake caused by about 80 km of active fault in the Nōbi fault zone moving all at once.[1] The Cabinet Office report calls it one of the largest earthquakes ever to happen inland in Japan.[2]

It killed 7,273 people, completely destroyed 142,177 houses and set off more than ten thousand landslides.[1] In the Neodani valley, where it started, almost every house collapsed. The University of Tokyo Library's panel makes a point I found striking: the earthquake came at dawn in the Meiji era, when many people were already up and about, so the number crushed to death was low compared with the number of houses that fell.[1]

> **Did you know?** I use 8.0 in the video, but some sources give 7.9. There was no modern seismometer network in 1891, so the magnitude has been estimated afterwards from the records of the time, and different sources land on slightly different numbers.

## Getting to Midori

![The small Tarumi Railway train at Midori station, with mountains and rice fields behind](/uploads/blog/neodani-train.jpg)

Midori is a small station in Neo, Motosu city, Gifu prefecture, on the Tarumi Railway. The line runs from Ōgaki to Tarumi,[3] so from Nagoya you take JR to Ōgaki first and change there. From Nagoya, with the change, it took me about two hours.

A few things are worth knowing before you go.

First, the Tarumi Railway doesn't take IC cards such as manaca or TOICA, so you need a paper ticket.[4] If you plan to get off at other stops along the line that day, there is a one-day pass for 1,800 yen (adults), sold at the counters at Ōgaki and Motosu stations.[5]

Second, trains are few. When I checked, there were only about ten a day from Midori back towards Ōgaki, and some of them run only at weekends and on holidays.[3] Check your train back before you set off, or you may wait over an hour at the station.

Third, Midori station is unstaffed.[3] The museum is a few minutes' walk away: the railway's website says two minutes and the Gifu tourism site says about five.[3][6]

![Near Midori station: deep countryside, mountains all round and hardly anyone about. The pointed roof on the right is the museum](/uploads/blog/neodani-museum.jpg)

My first thought on getting off the train was that I had really arrived in the countryside, what we would call kampung back home. Mountains everywhere, fresh air, and above all almost nobody around.

## The museum: Neodani Earthquake Fault Observation Museum

![The big roadside sign: Special Natural Monument, Neodani Fault, on this site](/uploads/blog/neodani-sign.jpg)

The museum's official name is 根尾谷地震断層観察館 (the Neodani Earthquake Fault Observation Museum), at 512 Neo-Midori, Motosu.[7] When I was there I had the place almost to myself.

According to Motosu city's website, from 1 April 2026 it opens from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. and is closed on Mondays (on the following day when Monday is a public holiday); hours may change with the season.[7] Adult admission is 500 yen. The earthquake experience hall next door costs another 200 yen, but the website says its machinery is under maintenance and it can't be used for now, so check before you go.[7]

### The hall underground is the real highlight

![Markers in the underground hall: 0 m starts here, and small red tags show where the layers have been offset](/uploads/blog/neodani-marker.jpg)

The museum has displays on earthquakes, some old seismometers and two small models, one showing a fault moving sideways and one showing it moving up. They are worth a look, but what makes this museum special is that they dug down into the ground where the fault runs and built a hall over it. Standing inside, you are looking straight at a cross-section of the fault.[6][7]

Look for the black layer of soil. It used to be one continuous layer, but the fault's movement has left one side higher than the other. The wall is marked at 0, 1, 3 and 6 metres to show you which way it moved. At university I had only ever seen fault cross-sections on lecture slides; this was the first time I saw a real one.

![Old seismometers on display in the museum](/uploads/blog/neodani-seismometers.jpg)

## Six metres up, four metres sideways: how an oblique fault moves

![Diagram: the fault pushed one side of the ground up by about 6 m and shifted it about 4 m sideways, cutting the road. The small figure is 1.7 m tall](/uploads/blog/neodani-slip.jpg)

At Midori the ground was offset by 6 metres vertically and 4 metres horizontally.[1] A fault that moves in both directions at once is called an oblique fault.

We usually picture faults as one of two kinds: one side goes up and the other down (dip-slip), or the two sides slide past each other sideways (strike-slip). Real faults often do a bit of both, and Midori is one of them. The Agency for Cultural Affairs describes it as a normal fault with a left-lateral component.[8] Left-lateral means that if you stand on one side and look across, the far side has moved to your left.

Put the two directions together and the two sides of the fault actually slid past each other on a slant. A bit of triangle arithmetic turns 6 m and 4 m into roughly 7 m. In other words, that morning this ground slid apart diagonally by more than two storeys.

The University of Tokyo panel includes an old photograph taken near Midori after the earthquake, with a road through the middle that the fault has cut and shifted.[1] The diagram in the video is drawn after that picture.

> **To be honest:** sources disagree on the horizontal figure. The University of Tokyo panel gives 4 m,[1] while the Tarumi Railway's page on the scarp near Midori station gives 3 m of sideways movement.[3] The 6 m vertical figure is the same everywhere. In both the video and this post I use the University of Tokyo numbers.

## Outside: the scarp and the viewing platform

![Looking down from the fault viewing platform: the raised slope running across the land is the fault](/uploads/blog/neodani-view.jpg)

Once you have seen the cross-section underground, do walk around outside. The step in the ground is still there outside the museum, but it is overgrown, and without the signs it isn't very obvious. When I stood beside the scarp and looked up, though, it was really tall.

![Me at the foot of the scarp. The grassy slope behind me is the ground that was pushed up in 1891](/uploads/blog/neodani-scarp.jpg)

Nearby there is a fault viewing platform, 断層展望広場, up a flight of steps. From the top you can see a raised line running across the land; that slope is the Neodani Fault. I'd suggest going up after you've seen the cross-section underground. Once you know what lies beneath, the line on the surface looks completely different.

### Why can you still see it after more than a century?

I gave my view in the video, and I'll spell it out here. This is my own reasoning, not the conclusion of any paper.

First, the vertical movement was large, about 6 metres, and a hundred-odd years of wind and rain haven't been enough to wear it down. Second, it was protected early. The Neodani Fault was designated a national Natural Monument in 1927,[8] and it is now a Special Natural Monument,[6][7] so it has been looked after ever since.

## What this has to do with Malaysia

On 24 August 2025, Segamat in Johor had a magnitude 4.1 earthquake.[9] Like the Nōbi earthquake it was an inland earthquake, meaning it started beneath the land rather than at an ocean trench.

The two magnitudes, though, are almost 4 apart. Each step up in magnitude releases roughly 32 times more energy,[10] so as a rough guide the Nōbi earthquake released several hundred thousand times the energy of the Segamat one. Treat that only as a ballpark figure, because the two numbers come from different agencies and different eras, worked out in different ways. Why you can't simply compare magnitudes from the news is the subject of "[Three things to ask when the news gives an earthquake's magnitude](/en/blog/reading-magnitude)", and the Segamat earthquake itself is in "[The Segamat Earthquakes](/en/blog/segamat-earthquake)".

Before I went to Midori I thought, like a lot of people, that a fault should be an obvious crack. Afterwards I realised it might be sitting in a patch of weeds, and unless someone marks it you could walk right past without noticing.

## Before you go

First, check the Tarumi Railway train back. There are only about ten a day, and some run only at weekends.

Second, bring cash or buy your ticket in advance, because the Tarumi Railway doesn't take IC cards.

Third, the museum is closed on Mondays and shuts at 4 p.m.; check whether the experience hall is open before you go.

Fourth, see the cross-section underground first, then go outside and up to the viewing platform to find the line on the surface.
