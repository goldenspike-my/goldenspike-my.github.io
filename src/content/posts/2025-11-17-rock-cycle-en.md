One of my favourite things in Kyoto is walking along the Kamo River. One day I was crouched on the bank looking at the stones, and it hit me that not a single one of them started out there.

Every one was washed down from upstream. They'll sit here for a while, and the next big flood will carry them further. Rocks only look still because they run on a completely different clock from us.

This is the extended version of my rock cycle video. I'll cover a few things there wasn't time for: who first came up with the idea, examples you can actually go and see in Malaysia and Kyoto, and a couple of places where I wasn't precise enough in the video and want to correct myself.

## What is the rock cycle?

The **rock cycle** is the way Earth's three families of rock, **igneous**, **sedimentary** and **metamorphic**, keep turning into one another.[1]

Two engines drive it:

- **Heat from inside the Earth**, which melts rock into magma, lifts up the crust and moves the plates.
- **Wind, water and ice at the surface**, powered ultimately by the Sun, which break rock down, carry it away and pile it up somewhere else.

There's no start and no finish.

![The rock cycle: the three families of rock and the routes between them](/uploads/blog/rock-cycle-en.png)

> Rocks do change. They just do it far more slowly than a human lifetime.

## Where the idea came from

![James Hutton, painted by Henry Raeburn (public domain, Wikimedia Commons) #small #right](/uploads/blog/photo-hutton.jpg)

The idea of Earth as a system that keeps recycling itself goes back to the Scottish naturalist **James Hutton**. He presented his theory to the Royal Society of Edinburgh in 1785 and published *Theory of the Earth* in 1788. He'd seen how rocks are worn down, laid down as sediment and lifted up again as new land, and he ended with a line that's still quoted today:

> "No vestige of a beginning, no prospect of an end."

That's why Hutton is often called the father of modern geology.[2]

> **A correction:** in the video I said "the present is the key to the past" was Hutton's own phrase. It's actually a later one-line summary of his thinking, usually credited to Archibald Geikie.[3] The idea is the same: use the processes we can watch today to read the rocks the past left behind.

## The three families, close to home

| Family | How it forms | Examples | Where you can see one |
|---|---|---|---|
| Igneous | magma or lava cools and hardens | granite, basalt | the granite of the Main Range |
| Sedimentary | fragments settle, get compacted and cemented | sandstone, limestone | sandstone on Langkawi; limestone at Batu Caves |
| Metamorphic | existing rock is changed by heat and pressure | marble, gneiss, slate | limestone becomes marble; granite becomes gneiss |

A few details I love:

**The backbone of the peninsula is granite.** The granite of the Main Range formed in the Late Triassic; zircon dating puts it at roughly 227 to 201 million years old.[4] Magma that cools slowly underground gives its minerals plenty of time to grow, so you can see granite's crystals with the naked eye. Lava that erupts and cools quickly forms fine-grained basalt instead.[1]

**Malaysia's oldest rocks are on Langkawi.** The Machinchang Formation is Cambrian sandstone, described as the oldest rock formation in the country, and it holds Malaysia's oldest known fossil, a trilobite.[5]

![The limestone cliffs and 272 steps of Batu Caves. Photo: CEphoto, Uwe Aranas, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0 #small #right](/uploads/blog/photo-batu-caves.jpg)

**Batu Caves is an old seabed.** The Kuala Lumpur Limestone that makes up Batu Caves is around 430 million years old, built from carbonate that piled up in a shallow Silurian sea.[6] When you climb those 272 steps, you're climbing up an ancient sea floor.

## Three roads a rock can take

### Road one: melt, then cool

Take any rock deep enough and hot enough and it can melt into **magma**. When that magma cools underground, or erupts as **lava** and cools at the surface, you get a new igneous rock.

### Road two: break down, then build up

Once rock is exposed at the surface, **weathering** and **erosion** go to work: wind, rain, rivers and glaciers grind it into fragments. That sand and mud is carried downhill and laid down layer by layer. As it gets buried deeper, the weight above squeezes it together and minerals cement the grains, and it becomes sedimentary rock.

I see this road every day in Kyoto. North-east of the city, between Mt Hiei and Mt Daimonji, there's a body of granite. As it weathers it breaks down into coarse white sand, which the Shirakawa River carries down: the famous **Shirakawa-suna**, or Shirakawa sand.[7][8] It's the white gravel raked into ripples in the dry gardens of so many Kyoto temples.

![The sand garden at Ginkaku-ji in Kyoto. The raked white sand in Kyoto's dry gardens is traditionally Shirakawa sand, weathered from granite. Photo: Jmhullot, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0](/uploads/blog/photo-ginkakuji-sand.jpg)

> **Did you know?** The raked sand in a Zen garden is literally halfway along road two: granite, weathered into sand. If a river carried it into a lake or the sea and it was buried deeply enough, in a few million years it could become a brand-new sandstone.

Sedimentary rocks have another special quality. Most of them form at fairly low temperature and pressure, so the remains of living things have a good chance of surviving. Almost all the fossils we find are in sedimentary rock.[1]

### Road three: baked and squeezed

When plate movements drag rock deep underground, or it ends up next to a body of magma, the temperature and pressure rise. Its minerals rearrange themselves or even turn into different minerals, but the rock **doesn't melt**. That's metamorphism: limestone becomes marble, shale becomes slate, granite becomes gneiss.

> **Another correction:** in the video I said metamorphism starts at about 150–200 °C and above roughly 1,000 bar. More accurately, the temperature at which metamorphism begins varies quite a lot from one rock type to another, so there's no single cut-off,[9] though textbooks often quote something in the region of one to two hundred degrees. There's no agreed minimum pressure either. Some metamorphism happens at very low pressure, such as rock baked right next to a magma body.

If the temperature keeps rising, metamorphic rock starts to melt, and we're back on road one.

## It isn't a one-way street

Textbook diagrams usually draw the rock cycle as a neat circle, which makes it look as if rocks have to go round in order. In reality any rock can take any road:

- Igneous rock can be weathered straight into sediment.
- Metamorphic rock can be lifted to the surface and eroded.
- Sedimentary rock can be buried deeper and metamorphosed, or even melted.

So it's less a circle and more a road map. Which road a rock takes depends on what happens to it along the way.

## Why it matters

**Resources.** Many ores form at particular stages of the cycle. Malaysia was once one of the world's great tin producers, and the granites of the Main Range are literally called the "Malaysian tin granites" in the research literature.[4] The sand, gravel and building stone we use all come from this cycle too.

**An archive of Earth's history.** Sedimentary rocks build up layer by layer, like a book written in order, recording past climates, sea levels and life.

**Scenery.** Mountains rising, canyons being cut, beaches forming: that's the cycle in motion. Next time you look at a mountain, a river or a beach, think about how much energy, time and pure chance it took to make it look the way it does today.
