A lot of Malaysian living rooms have a marble table or marble floor tiles. The shop sold it as marble, but what a geologist means by marble isn't always the same thing.

This episode is about how Ipoh's rough grey limestone hills became smooth, shiny marble. Here I want to come at it from the other side: if you have a piece of "marble" at home, how can you tell what it really is, and where it might have come from?

If you'd like the one-minute answer first, watch the episode's short, "Where do the wavy lines in marble come from?".

## Marble in the shop and marble in geology

To a geologist, marble is a metamorphic rock. Limestone is buried deep, heated and squeezed, and its calcite grows again into crystals that lock together. It's the same mineral as limestone, just grown differently.[1]

The stone trade uses the word much more loosely. Any crystalline carbonate rock that takes a polish can be sold as marble, and the name is sometimes used for travertine, serpentine and some limestones too.[2] So the "marble" in a showroom might be real marble, or it might be polished limestone.

![From the video: an ordinary limestone, with bits of shell when you look closely, and a sketch of its separate grains and gaps (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep05-grains.jpg)

![From the video: after heating and squeezing, the calcite grows again and locks together like a jigsaw, and the gaps disappear. That's marble (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep05-jigsaw.jpg)

## Three checks you can do at home

**First, look.** Ordinary limestone often still shows bits of shell up close, with gaps between the grains, and it feels rough. In marble the crystals are locked together, with no shells and hardly any gaps.[1] Zoom your phone camera right in on the edge of the table or a chipped corner; that's where it's easiest to see.

**Second, think about the scratches.** Marble is mostly calcite, which has a hardness of only 3, which is why it's so easy to carve.[2] Granite is full of quartz, which has a hardness of 7.[3][4] So a marble tabletop that's been used for years usually shows fine scratches, while a granite worktop hardly scratches at all. You don't need to test this yourself; just look at the table you already have.

**Third, a drop of vinegar, but not on the tabletop.** Marble reacts with acids,[2] so vinegar can dull the polished surface. If you want to try it, use a broken offcut or a sample from the shop. Vinegar only makes calcite give off very small bubbles, so a magnifying glass helps.[5]

![From the video: on the left, limestone fizzing with acid, which I filmed in the field at Batu Caves; on the right, a sketch showing that limestone and marble both fizz](/uploads/blog/ep05-acid.jpg)

> **To be honest:** the vinegar test only tells you there's calcite in it, not that it's marble. Limestone fizzes too. And some marble is made of dolomite, which barely reacts to a drop of cold acid unless you powder it first.[5] The marble at Simpang Pulai, near Ipoh, contains both calcite and dolomite.[6]

## What the veins are telling you

The lines running through marble are things in the original limestone that weren't calcite.[1] When limestone builds up on the seabed it often has some mud or iron-bearing minerals mixed in. Later, as the calcite grows back into crystals, these get pushed to the edges of the crystals, and as the rock is squeezed and deforms they are dragged out into wavy lines.[1][7]

![White marble: the grey veins are what was mixed into the original limestone (James St. John, CC BY 2.0)](/uploads/blog/ep05-marble.jpg)

So the whiter the marble and the fewer the veins, the cleaner the limestone it came from. Not every line forms this way, though. Some very straight, pale veins are calcite that grew later in cracks after the rock had already broken.[7]

## How old are Ipoh's rocks?

In the video I only said "hundreds of millions of years ago", because the sources genuinely disagree.

The limestone around Ipoh is called the Kinta Limestone. In 1995 Fontaine and Ibrahim gathered the fossils found in the Kinta Valley, and they range from the Middle Ordovician to the Middle Permian,[8] which is roughly 470 to 260 million years ago. Other studies put it in a narrower window.

The granite that baked it into marble is much younger. The Kinta Valley has the Kledang Range on its west side and the Main Range on its east,[9] and this granite cooled between about 227 and 201 million years ago.[10]

![Ipoh's limestone built up on the seabed over more than 200 million years, and granite baked it into marble later (schematic; data: Fontaine & Ibrahim 1995; Ng et al. 2015)](/uploads/blog/ep05-ipoh-timeline-en.png)

So Ipoh's rock went through two very different stages. First it built up slowly on the seabed for more than 200 million years. Then magma beside it cooled into granite and baked it into marble.

![From the video: the Kinta Valley lies between two ranges of granite, with Ipoh in the middle (schematic, not to scale)](/uploads/blog/ep05-kinta.jpg)

## Why there are almost no fossils in Ipoh's hills

Ordinary limestone is often built from shells, so it's full of fossils. But Fontaine and Ibrahim note that the limestone hills around Ipoh went through strong contact metamorphism and became crystalline limestone, with their fossils altered beyond recognition.[8] Fossils are calcite too, so when the rock was heated they grew into the crystals along with everything else, and their shapes were lost.

![From the video: ordinary limestone shows shells, but the hills around Ipoh have almost no recognisable fossils (schematic; source: Fontaine & Ibrahim 1995)](/uploads/blog/ep05-fossil.jpg)

![From the video: magma cools slowly into granite beneath the limestone, and the ring closest to it is baked into marble (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep05-oven.jpg)

> **To be honest:** the studies agree that Ipoh's marble was mainly baked by the granite next to it.[8][11] But a study in Ulu Chemor, further north, found that rock close to the granite wasn't always more strongly changed, so heat and pressure over a much wider area may have played a part there too.[12] And part of the Kinta Limestone has been interpreted as forming on a deeper slope of the seabed, not only in shallow sea.[9] I left all of this out of the video because none of it is settled.

## Tin, hills and caves

The same granite that brought the heat also brought the tin. As the magma was close to cooling, hot water carrying tin escaped along cracks in the rock and left tin ore in them.[13] Later the rock weathered and wore away, and the heavy tin stayed behind in the sand and mud of the valley, which is where people dug it out.[14]

![The tin mine at Tambun, about 1910 (C.J. Kleingrothe, KITLV, public domain)](/uploads/blog/ep05-tambun-tin.jpg)

Ipoh's steep, isolated hills are what geologists call tower karst.[11] Rainwater is very slightly acidic, and over time it dissolves limestone,[1] carving out caves and sheer walls. I talked about that in my Batu Caves short, "[Why is Batu Caves hollow?](https://youtube.com/shorts/8nFchSoMsPE)".

## Where to see it for yourself

**Ipoh's limestone hills.** Look around from almost anywhere in Ipoh: the steep, plant-covered hills rising out of the flat valley are the ones. Next time you visit the cave temples, look at the rock walls around you.

**The marble road at Simpang Pulai.** Just outside Ipoh, Simpang Pulai has a road lined with marble factories and showrooms making tabletops, tombstones and tiles.[15] The marble there is quarried from the rock nearby.[6]

**The Lake of the Pregnant Maiden in Langkawi.** The hills beside Tasik Dayang Bunting are limestone turned into marble by heat from below, and the area is one of Langkawi Geopark's Geoforest Parks.[16][17]

## Three questions for the next piece of marble you see

First, is it marble in the geological sense or in the shop's sense? Look for shells, and check whether the crystals lock together.

Second, are the lines wavy or straight? Wavy lines are mud and iron squeezed out of the old limestone; straight ones may be calcite that grew later in a crack.

Third, where did it come from? If it's from around Ipoh, it built up on the seabed for over 200 million years and was then baked by granite before it ever became your table.
