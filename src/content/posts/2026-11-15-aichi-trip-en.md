I went to Nagoya for the Seismological Society of Japan (SSJ) meeting, to present my poster. Once the conference was over, I spent a few days wandering around Aichi and Gifu.

The vlog goes through the trip one day at a time and ends with my answer to whether Nagoya is worth a visit. Here I want to answer a different question: if you're spending a few days in Nagoya, how do you plan them so you don't wear yourself out or waste time? There are a few things I only found out once I was there, and knowing them earlier would have saved me a lot of trouble.

## My answer first

A day or two in the city, and a day or two in the countryside. Nagoya is a big city, and Nagoya Castle, Ōsu and the MIRAI TOWER are all worth seeing, but at weekends it's really crowded. Places like Inuyama and Midori are quiet, the air is fresh, and you see a different side of Japan. Looking back on the five days, the countryside is what stayed with me most.

## Inuyama: check which station your ticket says

![A Meitetsu QR ticket on my phone, reading Meitetsu Nagoya to Inuyama](/uploads/blog/aichi-ticket.jpg)

From Nagoya you can take the Meitetsu line to Inuyama. Meitetsu sells an Inuyama Jōkamachi ticket that covers the return train, entry to Inuyama Castle, a discount at the Uraku-en garden and coupons for shops in the old town. The paper version from Meitetsu Nagoya costs ¥1,950 for an adult and is valid for two consecutive days.[1]

I bought mine on my phone. That morning I wanted to see the rocks by the river first, so I rode straight to Inuyama-Yūen, the closer station. At the gate I found I couldn't get out. I asked the station staff, who said I couldn't leave there, and with a QR ticket I couldn't even pay the difference. Only then did I notice that the ticket said Inuyama, the stop before.

When I looked it up afterwards, I found that the phone version only works at Meitetsu Nagoya and Inuyama stations, while the paper one does go as far as Inuyama-Yūen.[1] So check which station is printed on the ticket before you buy. If you plan to start at the Inuyama-Yūen end, the paper ticket is the easier choice.

![The old town in Inuyama, with wooden houses on both sides of the street](/uploads/blog/aichi-jokamachi.jpg)

Because I'd overshot, my whole day ran backwards: old town first, then the castle, and the river last. It actually worked quite well. The old town is a single historic street, very relaxed, and you can see the castle from it. At the far end are two shrines, Sankō Inari and Haritsuna, and they couldn't be more different. Sankō Inari has a row of red torii gates; Haritsuna is plainer and more rustic. I also kept running into Taiwanese visitors, hearing their accent every few steps.

![Looking down from the top of Inuyama Castle, with the Kiso River winding off towards the plain](/uploads/blog/aichi-kisogawa.jpg)

Inuyama Castle is one of Japan's five national-treasure castles, and its keep is original rather than a modern rebuild.[2] From the top you can see the whole sweep of the Kiso River. Afterwards I walked along the river to the iron bridge, which gives you the castle from one angle after another. The rocks by the river are unusual, and I've made a separate video about them.

## Midori: look up the train back before you set off

![The small Tarumi Railway train waiting at a station in the mountains](/uploads/blog/aichi-tarumi.jpg)

Midori is in Gifu, and it's where I went for my video on the Neodani fault. From Nagoya you take JR to Ōgaki and change to the Tarumi Railway. It runs a single carriage and doesn't take IC cards, so you need a paper ticket.[3] Only about ten trains a day leave Midori for Ōgaki.[3]

When I went, the next train back was at least an hour and a half after the one I arrived on. So before you go, look up which train you'll take home, because if you miss it you'll be waiting another hour and a half. The station keeps umbrellas you can borrow, so a sudden shower isn't a problem.

How to get there, what to see in the museum and how the fault moved are all in my post [Visiting the Neodani Fault at Midori](/blog/neodani-fault).

## Stop at Ōgaki on the way back

![A canal in Ōgaki, with groundwater pouring straight out of the stone wall](/uploads/blog/aichi-ogaki-water.jpg)

You pass through Ōgaki on the way back from Midori anyway, so I'd get off and have a look around. Ōgaki is known as the city of water: there's so much groundwater that you can see it welling up by the side of the street.

Ōgaki Castle was a base for Ishida Mitsunari and the Western army at the time of the Battle of Sekigahara. It burned down in an air raid in 1945, and the keep you see today was rebuilt in 1959.[4] Entry is only ¥200 anyway, and the day I went fell during a culture week, so it was free. Inside you can try drawing a bow and lifting an old matchlock gun. I tried holding one up with one hand, and it was really heavy.

## Central Nagoya: at weekends, be ready to queue

![The keep of Nagoya Castle, currently closed to visitors](/uploads/blog/aichi-nagoya-castle.jpg)

Having come all the way to Nagoya, I had to see Nagoya Castle. It's the biggest and best known of the castles on this trip, but the keep is closed at the moment because it isn't earthquake-resistant enough, so you can only photograph it from outside.[5] Next to it, the Honmaru Palace was restored to its original form in 2018, with gilded paintings covering the walls. There's no extra charge for the palace, but you need a Nagoya Castle ticket, and it's open from 9 am to 4 pm.[6]

I went at the weekend and the queue for the palace was long. As I slowly made my way in, the staff's estimate went from 40 minutes to 70. I'm not especially into palaces, so I wasn't going to wait over an hour, and just peeked in through a window. If you really want to go inside, go early on a weekday.

To be honest, after a while castles start to look alike. I saw Inuyama, Ōgaki and Nagoya castles one after another, and by Nagoya I was feeling a bit numb. If you're short of time, pick one or two.

![The entrance to the Ōsu shopping street](/uploads/blog/aichi-osu.jpg)

After the castle you can head to the Ōsu shopping street. It's a bit like old Nagoya mixed with Akihabara: lots of food, second-hand anime shops and second-hand clothes. At the weekend it was packed too.

![The MIRAI TOWER lit up at night](/uploads/blog/aichi-mirai.jpg)

In the evening, go and see the MIRAI TOWER. It was Japan's first tower to carry several broadcasters' transmitters together, and in 2022 it was designated an Important Cultural Property.[7] It photographs well when it's lit up at night. The evening I went it was only 13 or 14 degrees, so bring a jacket.

If you're getting around central Nagoya by subway and bus at the weekend, look at the Donichi Eco ticket, a day pass that's only valid at weekends and on holidays.[8] That's what I used that day.

## The last day: Atsuta Shrine and the science museum

Atsuta Shrine holds the Kusanagi sword, one of Japan's three imperial regalia, and it's said to rank second only to Ise.[9] Being a sacred treasure, though, the sword is kept out of sight.

![Signatures on display at the Nagoya City Science Museum, with a note that the TV drama Galileo was filmed there](/uploads/blog/aichi-galileo.jpg)

After lunch I went to the Nagoya City Science Museum (now called FUJI Nagoya Science Museum). The biggest surprise was the TV drama *Galileo*. Masaharu Fukuyama and Yūki Amami have a scene in a museum full of dinosaur fossils, and I had no idea it was this one. Their signatures are on display. I really like that show, so I got a bit excited.

The museum is best known for its giant silver sphere, which holds a planetarium certified by Guinness World Records, with a dome 35 metres across inside.[10] There's also a tornado lab where an artificial vortex 9 metres tall spins up, and it really does look like a tornado.[10]

![The tornado lab at the science museum, with an artificial vortex rising from the floor](/uploads/blog/aichi-tornado.jpg)

The exhibition halls alone cost ¥500 for an adult, or ¥1,000 with the planetarium. It's closed on Mondays and on the third Friday of each month, moving to another day when those fall on a holiday.[11]

> **A tip:** there was no bus or subway from the museum to my hotel, so after picking up my luggage I walked about two kilometres. If you'll be carrying bags on your last day too, work out in advance how to get from the museum to the station.

## If I planned it again

I'd booked a 7.30 pm shinkansen, but in the end I was so tired I just went back to Kyoto early. The trip was certainly full, but if I did it again I'd leave more room.

Pick one or two cities and castles, and give the time you save to places like Inuyama and Midori. On the countryside days, look up the train back first and check which station your ticket says. Nagoya is still worth visiting. I just don't think its best parts are necessarily in the city.
