At 6.13 on Sunday morning, 24 August 2025, a lot of people in Segamat woke up to their house shaking.

MetMalaysia soon reported a magnitude 4.1 earthquake with its epicentre about 5 km west of Segamat.[1] Less than three hours later there was a magnitude 2.8 near Yong Peng.[2] Over the following week came more: 3.2, 3.4 and 2.7 on the 27th, 29th and 30th.[3]

The message I got most that day was: "I thought Malaysia didn't have earthquakes?"

This post is the extended version of the video. The video covers the two types of earthquake and a fairly bold idea of mine about what caused this one. Here I've added more background: whether Malaysia has had quakes like this before, how scientists explain earthquakes in the middle of a plate, and how confident I actually am about my idea.

## What happened

| Date (2025) | Magnitude (MetMalaysia) | Location |
|---|---|---|
| 24 Aug, 06:13 | 4.1 | about 5 km west of Segamat |
| 24 Aug, 09:00 | 2.8 | about 28 km north-west of Kluang (near Yong Peng) |
| 27 Aug | 3.2 | about 18 km south of Segamat |
| 29 Aug | 3.4 | Segamat area |
| 30 Aug | 2.7 | Segamat area |

![Earthquakes around Segamat in August 2025, with magnitudes as reported by MetMalaysia](/uploads/blog/segamat-sequence-en.png)

None of these were big, but they were close to the surface, so they were clearly felt. When JKR later inspected government buildings in the area, 15 had minor damage, including cracks in school and hospital walls. Most were old cracks that had opened up a bit more, and none affected the structures' safety.[4]

## So does Malaysia really get no earthquakes?

Not quite. Before Segamat, you have to go back to 1922 for Johor's last significant recorded earthquakes: one on 31 January and another on 7 February, estimated at around magnitude 5.4 and 5.0, with reports of minor damage at the time.[5]

> **Did you know?** There was no modern seismometer network in 1922. Those magnitudes were worked out backwards from historical reports of how strongly the shaking was felt in different places. The same study went through old newspapers and archives and found at least 34 felt earthquakes between 1803 and 1950 that may have originated in or near the Malay Peninsula.[5]

Another study compiled the earthquakes of Peninsular Malaysia from 1922 to 2020 and found 59 events, ranging from Mw 0.7 to 5.4, most of them shallow.[6]

So the accurate version is: Malaysia gets **few earthquakes, and they're usually small**. But not zero.

## Two kinds of earthquake

The big earthquakes we see on the news from Japan or Indonesia are mostly **interplate earthquakes**: they happen where two plates grind against each other. Segamat was an **intraplate earthquake**, one that happens inside a plate.

| | Interplate | Intraplate |
|---|---|---|
| Where | at plate boundaries | inside a plate |
| Examples | Japan, Indonesia, the Philippines | Segamat, Johor in 1922 |
| How often | very often | rarely |
| Where the energy comes from | plates pushing directly on each other | stress transmitted from distant plate motion, plus old faults moving again |

![Left: an interplate earthquake, where two plates push against each other. Right: an intraplate earthquake on an old fault inside a plate (schematic)](/uploads/blog/plate-types-en.png)

The Malay Peninsula sits on Sundaland, a fairly stable piece of continental crust, five to six hundred kilometres from the nearest plate boundary, the subduction zone off Sumatra.[6] The push from those distant plates slowly makes its way inland and loads up some very old faults. When one of them can't hold any more, it slips.

## How a fault gets nudged

Seismologists usually split the triggers into two kinds.[7]

**Static stress.** When a fault slips, it permanently changes how the surrounding rock is loaded. Some areas are relieved, others are squeezed harder, and faults in the squeezed areas are more likely to go next. That's why aftershocks follow a main shock, a bit like one domino knocking over the next.

**Dynamic stress.** Seismic waves from a big earthquake far away pass through and briefly push and pull on local faults. If a fault is already close to its limit, that jolt can be enough. This kind of remote triggering can happen thousands of kilometres from the original quake.

In the video I pointed out that there hadn't been any unusually large distant earthquake just before Segamat, so I thought it was unlikely the passing waves of a far-off quake had set it off. Which leaves the question: what else?

## My idea: hot water underground

Please read this part as **my hypothesis**, not an established result.

Whether a fault slips depends on how tightly the rock on either side is pressed together. If water gets into the fault, its pressure pushes the two sides apart very slightly, the clamping force drops, and the fault can slip more easily. Geologists call this the **pore pressure** effect, and it's a classic idea in fault mechanics.[8]

> It isn't that water makes the fault slippery. The water pressure props the two sides apart, just a little.

![Pore pressure: once water gets into a fault, its pressure props the sides apart slightly and the force clamping the fault drops (schematic)](/uploads/blog/pore-pressure-en.png)

Is there water down there? Malaysia has plenty of hot springs, and they're all **non-volcanic**: rainwater soaks down through faults and fractures to great depth, is heated by the warm rock, and rises again. The Geological Society of Malaysia notes that many of them are linked to granite bodies and fault zones.[9][10]

There are hot springs at Labis, not far from Segamat. The village nearby is even called Air Panas, "hot water".[11] Hot springs tell us that heated water really is circulating along faults below the surface.

### A Japanese example: the Noto Peninsula

From late 2020, the Noto Peninsula in Ishikawa had an earthquake swarm that went on for years. When researchers relocated the earthquakes precisely, they found the activity spreading outwards and upwards over time, the way fluid diffuses through cracks in rock.[12][13] On 1 January 2024 the area was hit by an M7.6 earthquake, and how much the fluids had to do with it is still being actively researched.[14]

My thought was that Segamat might be a smaller version of the same thing: fluid moving along a fault that was already loaded, letting it slip, with the smaller quakes afterwards following on through static stress, domino-style.

> **To be honest:** proving this would need more seismometers around Segamat, so that every aftershock can be located accurately in three dimensions and we can see whether they migrate the way fluid-driven swarms do. The data we have isn't good enough yet. MetMalaysia, for its part, attributed the quakes to renewed movement on the Mersing Fault Zone.[15] The two explanations don't necessarily contradict each other: fluid could be one of the reasons an old fault started moving again.

## A correction: that "10 km depth"

In the video I repeated the news and said this earthquake was 10 km deep. When I was researching my episode on epicentres, I checked the USGS record and found its depth listed as *operator assigned*, meaning there wasn't enough data and a default value was entered by hand. USGS also put the epicentre about 13 km east-south-east of Segamat, more than ten kilometres away from MetMalaysia's location.[16]

![The Segamat epicentre according to the two agencies. The USGS location comes from USGS ComCat (us6000r3fi); MetMalaysia only published "about 5 km west of Segamat", so its point is placed from that description. Map: Natural Earth](/uploads/blog/segamat-map-en.png)

So the "10 km" wasn't necessarily measured, and the epicentre itself carries an uncertainty of over ten kilometres. That matters a lot if you're trying to work out which fault was responsible. There's more on this in [my post on epicentres and hypocentres](/en/blog/epicentre-hypocentre).

## So, are we safe?

The Segamat earthquakes were small, and Peninsular Malaysia is still a low-seismicity region overall. A handful of quakes won't turn it into Japan or Indonesia.

They do remind us of two things, though. Earthquakes can happen inside plates too, and because they tend to be shallow, even small ones can crack older buildings. And we still know surprisingly little about the faults and fluids under the peninsula. More seismometers and a better look at the historical record would do far more good than worrying.
