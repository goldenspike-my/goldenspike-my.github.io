On my desk there's a small piece of sandstone with a label that says "11". Run a fingernail over it and it feels rough, but not a single grain comes off.

It does remember being sand, though. The video is about how sand becomes rock. In this post I want to come at it from the other end: if you're holding a piece of sandstone, what can you read from it yourself?

If you'd like the short answer first, here's [the one-minute version](https://youtube.com/shorts/S6KS72XDTRs).

## First look: the grains under a lens

![My sandstone sample, number 11. The surface is rough and you can see the individual grains](/uploads/blog/ep04-sample.jpg)

Take a hand lens, or zoom right in with your phone camera, and look at a broken face of the sandstone. You'll see grain after grain of roughly the same size, packed against each other. That's the first sign it was once sand.

Malaysia's oldest rocks, the Machinchang Formation in Langkawi, are exactly this kind of rock: mostly sandstone with some shale and mudstone, more than 500 million years old, laid down by ancient rivers and seas.[1]

![From the video: over 500 million years ago, a river carries sand to its mouth and spreads it in a fan across the sea floor (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep04-delta.jpg)

> **Did you know?** Not all of the Machinchang sandstone is sand left untouched. Some of it was later lightly metamorphosed into a harder metaquartzite sandstone, where the boundaries between the grains are less sharp.[2] Calling it "rock made of sand" is still fair; it just hasn't spent those 500 million years lying quietly.

## The ripples are talking: waves or a river?

![Fossil ripple marks in a sandstone in Britain (Gordon Hatton, CC BY-SA 2.0)](/uploads/blog/ep04-fossil-ripples.jpg)

![Ripples left by water on a beach today (Stephan Sprinz, CC BY 4.0). The shape is almost the same as the rock above, hundreds of millions of years older](/uploads/blog/ep04-beach-ripples.jpg)

The Machinchang rocks show ripple marks, along with the tracks and burrows of ancient animals.[1] In the video I only said they look just like the ripples on a beach today, but a ripple can tell you more than that: how the water was moving.

Look at a ripple in cross-section. If both sides slope about equally, it's **symmetrical**, which means the water was swinging back and forth, usually as waves. If one side is gentle and the other steep, it's **asymmetrical**, which means the water was flowing one way, as in a river or a tidal current, and the steep side faces downstream.[3][4]

So the ripples on a rock can tell you whether that spot, half a billion years ago, was more like a beach or more like a river channel. Next time you see ripples at the beach or by a river, make a guess first, then check which way the water is going.

## Half of it is empty: what squeezing can do

![From the video: new layers of sand and mud land on top and the sand underneath gets thinner; in the magnifier the grains are squeezed closer and the water is pushed out (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep04-compaction.jpg)

Freshly laid sand is roughly half empty space, with water in the gaps.[5] As it gets buried, the weight above pushes the grains together, squeezes the water out, and the gaps shrink. Sandstones along the US Gulf Coast are a well-known example: at 0.4 km down they still have about 35% pore space, and by 4.5 km only about 11%.[6]

Smaller gaps don't mean the grains are stuck, though. You can try the experiment from the video: squeeze a handful of dry sand as hard as you like, open your hand, and it still falls apart.

![From the video: dry sand falls apart when you let go; add a little water and it holds together (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep04-dry-wet.jpg)

## Why a sandcastle doesn't last until tomorrow

![A sandcastle on the beach at Benidorm, Spain (Diego Delso, CC BY-SA 3.0)](/uploads/blog/ep04-sandcastle.jpg)

Add a little water and the sand will hold a shape, because the surface tension of the thin water films between the grains pulls them together. A group of physicists who set out to build the strongest possible sandcastle found you don't need much: about 1% water does it.[7] Once the water dries, though, nothing holds the grains, and the castle only lasts until the sun gets to it.

To turn sand into rock you need a glue that doesn't disappear when it dries.

## The vinegar test: what glue holds your sandstone together?

![From the video: white mineral grows outward from the surface of each grain until the gaps are filled (schematic; sand grains are roughly 0.1 to 2 mm)](/uploads/blog/ep04-cement.jpg)

That glue is minerals that groundwater leaves between the grains, a process geologists call cementation. The commonest cements are calcite (calcium carbonate), quartz, iron oxides and some clay minerals, and iron oxide is often what gives sandstone a yellow or reddish tint.[8][9]

Calcite is the same stuff as the scale at the bottom of a kettle. Water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium, and when hard water is heated, calcium carbonate comes out as a hard deposit. It's also why some people run vinegar through their coffee maker: vinegar is an acid, and it dissolves the scale.[10]

That gives you an experiment you can do at home. Put a few drops of white vinegar on your sandstone and look closely with a lens. If the cement is calcite, you'll see tiny bubbles, and sometimes a grain or two comes loose; vinegar only reacts weakly, so you need to get close.[11] If nothing happens, the glue may be quartz or iron oxide, which vinegar can't touch.

In the field, geologists use dilute hydrochloric acid, which reacts much more strongly, but that calls for safety glasses and gloves. At home, vinegar is enough.[11]

> **Did you know?** Kettle scale and the cement underground are the same chemical, but they form in different ways. In a kettle the water is heated, carbon dioxide escapes, and calcium carbonate comes out quickly. Nobody boils anything underground: there the mineral grows a little at a time, kilometres down.

## Why does the glue need to be kilometres down?

![From the video: on the left, boiling water and fast-growing scale; on the right, several kilometres down, where the cement grows slowly (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep04-slow.jpg)

If the cement is quartz, temperature matters a lot. The sandstones under the North Sea oil fields have been studied in great detail: large amounts of quartz cement usually only start forming below 2 to 3 km, at around 80 to 100 °C.[12] Another North Sea study puts the onset at about 2.7 km and 80 °C.[13] The hotter it is, the faster quartz dissolves, moves and grows again.

That's also why beach sand usually stays sand: it never gets buried. The waves keep moving it around, and the cement never gets the chance to grow.

## Rock in about 150 days

![Beachrock on Chrysi island, Greece: the beach sand has been cemented into slabs of rock (Quim Gil, CC0)](/uploads/blog/ep04-beachrock.jpg)

There are exceptions. On some tropical and subtropical coasts with a small tidal range, in the part of the beach the waves reach, calcium carbonate cement grows between the grains unusually fast and turns the sand into slabs of rock called beachrock.[15]

A study published in 2025 put this to a direct test. On Little Ambergris Cay in the Turks and Caicos, the team left small mesh pouches of sterilised sand in the upper intertidal zone. The first traces of cement between the grains appeared after just 4 days, and after 5 months the grains had stuck into clumps up to about 1 cm across, held by the cement together with filaments of cyanobacteria. Their conclusion: fresh rock can form in as little as 150 days.[16] The beachrock there even has 20th-century rubbish cemented into it.[16]

One caveat: the sand there is made of ooids, small rounded grains of calcium carbonate, not the quartz sand most of us know, and microbes help along the way. It's a very different setting from cementation several kilometres underground.

> **To be honest:** in the video I said sand "often takes millions of years" to become sandstone. That's the usual answer, but I couldn't find a figure that holds everywhere. The Smithsonian puts it at "thousands or millions of years", and some sandstone is so poorly cemented it falls back into sand when you dig it out.[14] Add the 150-day beachrock and the honest answer is: it depends on how fast the glue grows, and that depends on temperature, water and the minerals involved.

## What happened to Machinchang next

![From the video: the sandstone is squeezed and pushed up into a mountain, then worn down by rain and waves, and the sand washes back to the coast (schematic)](/uploads/blog/ep04-rock-cycle.jpg)

Over 500 million years ago the Machinchang sand was buried, squeezed and cemented into sandstone. Later, forces deep underground pushed it up into the mountain we see today, and now rain and waves are breaking it down again and washing it back to the sea. How rocks get pushed up involves plate tectonics and mountain building, which I'm saving for an episode of its own. The whole loop is in my earlier post, [Earth's Recycling Plan: The Three Lives of a Rock](/en/blog/rock-cycle).

![Machinchang, Langkawi (DerYang2004, CC BY-SA 4.0)](/uploads/blog/ep04-machinchang.jpg)

## Four things to ask yourself at the beach next time

First, do the ripples on the sand slope the same on both sides? Equal slopes mean waves; one steep side means a current, with the steep side facing downstream.

Second, could the sand you're standing on ever get buried? Sand that the waves keep moving never gets the chance to cement, unless it's one of those tropical beaches where beachrock grows.

Third, do the rocks nearby fizz with a drop of vinegar? If they do, their glue is probably calcite, the same stuff as kettle scale.

Fourth, could some of the sand in your hand have been broken off a mountain like Machinchang by rain and waves?
