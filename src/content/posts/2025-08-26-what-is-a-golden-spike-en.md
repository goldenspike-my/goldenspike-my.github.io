When I started the channel, the question I got most wasn't "what do you study?" It was "what kind of name is 金釘子?" To a lot of people it sounds like something from a hardware shop, or a hidden weapon in a kung fu novel.

It's actually a proper geological term. I only had three minutes for it in the video, so here's the longer version: where golden spikes are, what they look like, and why one of them has been argued over for years and still hasn't been driven in.

## Earth's history needs a ruler

Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, and geologists divide that time into chunks: the Cambrian, the Jurassic, the Cretaceous and so on. You've probably heard a few of these in dinosaur films.

Here's the catch. Where exactly does one chunk start?

If every country defined the Jurassic using its own rocks, Malaysia might put the start at one layer and Japan at another, and nobody's results would line up. It's like two friends agreeing to meet "in the afternoon": one turns up at two, the other at five.

What you need is a single reference that everyone accepts. Somewhere in a real rock section, you point at one level and say: below this is the older period, above it is the younger one.

> A golden spike is a mark on the geological time scale, and it's driven into actual rock.

## GSSP: the official name

The formal term is **Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point**, or **GSSP**. It's a mouthful, but each part means something simple:

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| Global Boundary | a time boundary used worldwide |
| Stratotype Section | the sequence of rock layers chosen as the reference |
| Point | the exact level in that section |

To choose the point, geologists look for a signal that can be recognised around the world, such as the first appearance of a particular fossil or a sharp change in the rock's chemistry. That way, someone working on rocks on the other side of the planet can find the same moment in time without ever visiting the reference site.

> **Did you know?** Golden spikes are rarely golden and often aren't spikes. Many sites are marked with a bronze plaque or a metal disc. The name is borrowed from the golden spike that finished the first transcontinental railway in the United States.

A boundary only becomes official once the International Commission on Stratigraphy (ICS) has reviewed it and the International Union of Geological Sciences (IUGS) has ratified it. On the ICS International Chronostratigraphic Chart, every boundary that has one is marked with a tiny golden spike icon.[1][2]

## A few spikes with a story

### The first one: Klonk, Czech Republic

The first golden spike ever ratified is on Klonk hill near Prague. It was approved in 1972 at the International Geological Congress in Montreal and marks the boundary between the Silurian and the Devonian. The signal is the first appearance of a graptolite called *Monograptus uniformis*.[3]

Half a century later, the ICS list has roughly 80 ratified golden spikes.[1]

### The worst extinction: Meishan, China

The boundary between the Permian and the Triassic marks the biggest mass extinction in Earth's history. Its golden spike is at Meishan in Changxing, Zhejiang, ratified in 2001, placed where the conodont *Hindeodus parvus* first appears.[4]

### Exit the dinosaurs: El Kef, Tunisia

The boundary between the Cretaceous and the Paleogene, when the non-bird dinosaurs disappeared, has its golden spike near El Kef in Tunisia, ratified in 1991.[1]

> **What about Malaysia?** I went through the ICS list and couldn't find a golden spike in Malaysia. We do have very old rocks, though. The Machinchang Formation on Langkawi is Cambrian sandstone and is described as the oldest rock formation in the country, and in 2007 Langkawi became the first UNESCO Global Geopark in Southeast Asia.[5]

## The spike that didn't get driven in: the Anthropocene

Golden spikes aren't handed out because an idea is popular. The Anthropocene shows this better than anything.

A working group argued that human impact on the planet is now big enough to start a new geological epoch. They proposed Crawford Lake in Canada, where the lake mud preserves the plutonium fallout from 1950s nuclear tests, as the site for the spike.[7]

In March 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted it down: 12 against, 4 in favour and 3 abstentions.[6] "Anthropocene" is still a useful way to talk about human impact, but it isn't a formal unit of geological time.

I think that sums up what a golden spike is for. It needs evidence anyone in the world can check and match, not just a good story.
