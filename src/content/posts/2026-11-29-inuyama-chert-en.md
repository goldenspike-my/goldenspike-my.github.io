In the video I asked whether the rocks under Inuyama Castle were once on the deep seabed. They were. It's just that they took a very, very long time to ride a plate all the way to the foot of the castle.

Here I want to cover what the video ran out of time for. If you'd like to see them yourself, where should you go and how do you recognise them? And where does the "one layer of chert plus one of shale takes about 20,000 years on average" in the video actually come from?

## Where to see it

![Inuyama Castle on its hill, seen from the bank of the Kiso River](/uploads/blog/inuyama-castle.jpg)

Inuyama Castle is one of the five castles in Japan whose keep is a National Treasure,[1] and most visitors spend their time looking up at it. Walk down to the Kiso River below the castle, though, and the rocks are right there. When the Ministry of the Environment describes the middle reaches of the Kiso, it singles out the chert here and its "complex folding".[2]

I walked along the river towards Inuyama Bridge. Even from a distance, the cliff beside the bridge shows layer upon layer of rock squeezed into bends, and under the railway bridge it's even clearer. Kimura of the Geological Survey of Japan notes that around Inuyama the rock is exposed almost continuously for about 4 km along the Kiso.[3]

![The cliff beside Inuyama Bridge: you can see the folded layers from across the water](/uploads/blog/inuyama-cliff.jpg)

From Nagoya you can take the Meitetsu line. If you buy the phone version of the Inuyama Jōkamachi ticket, note that it only works at Meitetsu Nagoya and Inuyama stations: you can't break your journey on the way or ride past your stop.[4] I rode one stop too far, to Inuyama-Yūen, and couldn't get out of the gate; that story is in the vlog.

Some of the rock by the water is slippery, so wear shoes you can walk in. I only touched the rocks with my hand; I didn't hammer anything or take anything away.

## How to recognise chert

![Running a hand over the chert, you can feel each layer](/uploads/blog/inuyama-hand.jpg)

Chert is a very hard, very fine-grained rock. What you see at Inuyama is bedded chert: a layer of chert a few centimetres thick, a thin layer of shale, more chert, and so on, over and over.[5] Run your hand over it and you can feel the edges of the layers. The colours range from reddish brown to grey and black; in the video I said this probably has to do with the minerals in the rock and how weathered it is.

![Chert by the river, with a yellow scale bar: none of the layers is thick](/uploads/blog/inuyama-scale.jpg)

It isn't like sandstone, which is made from sand that rivers carry. Chert is mostly the skeletons of tiny deep-sea organisms that sank and piled up on the seabed, layer by layer. Research in this area dates the rocks using the radiolarian fossils inside the chert.[6] These rocks were laid down in the deep sea more than 200 million years ago, from the Triassic into the Early Jurassic.[3][5][6]

> **Did you know?** Ikeda and colleagues are clear that this bedded chert built up in the open ocean, below the "carbonate compensation depth".[5] Put simply, the sea was so deep that anything made of calcium carbonate, such as shells, dissolved before it reached the bottom. Only the tiny silica skeletons were left to pile up.

## How long one layer takes: where 20,000 years comes from

In the video I said that research shows one layer of chert plus one of shale takes about 20,000 years on average. That figure comes from a 2010 paper by Ikeda, Tada and Sakuma, which studied exactly this chert: the Middle Triassic sequence at Inuyama.[5]

They measured the chert layer by layer, dated the sequence from its fossils, and worked out that each chert-and-shale pair represents about 20,000 years on average. That happens to match the precession cycle, the slow wobble of the Earth's axis. When they analysed how the layer thicknesses change through the sequence, they also found longer cycles, of about 100,000 and 400,000 years, which match the slow changes in the Earth's orbit too.[5] Their conclusion is that these layers were laid down in step with the Earth's orbital rhythm.

> **To be honest:** 20,000 years is an average. An earlier study (Hori and colleagues, 1993) put one pair at somewhere between 10,000 and 70,000 years, with a median of 20,000 years for the Late Triassic and 40,000 for the Early Jurassic.[5] So when I said in the video that the little stretch I touched might already span hundreds of thousands of years, I meant a dozen or so pairs added together, not that every layer is the same length.

## How deep-sea rock ended up under a castle

![From the video: the ocean plate dives under the continent, and the chert on top is scraped off and stacked, slice by slice, against the edge of the continent until it becomes today's Inuyama (diagram, not to scale)](/uploads/blog/inuyama-accretion.jpg)

You might think the sea simply drew back and the seabed became land. That isn't what happened.

Much of the ground beneath the Japanese islands is accretionary complex: rock that slowly built up where an ocean plate meets a continental one.[7] The ocean plate keeps diving under the continent, but the deep-sea chert on top of it can't go down with it, so it is scraped off and stacked, slice by slice, against the edge of the continent. The Mino Belt, where Inuyama sits, was built this way from the Jurassic to the Early Cretaceous.[7]

Wakita's conference abstract adds a lovely detail: around Inuyama the rocks run from chert at the bottom, through siliceous shale and mudstone, to sandstone at the top, getting coarser as they go up.[7] It reads like a travel log of the plate on its way from the deep ocean to the trench. Far from land there were only tiny deep-sea skeletons; the closer it came to the trench, the more mud and sand washed down from the land.

## How can rock this hard bend?

![The cliff under the railway bridge, where the layers of chert are bent into waves](/uploads/blog/inuyama-under-bridge.jpg)

Chert is very hard, but it wasn't bent in one go. Kimura divides the deformation here into two stages. First, during accretion, thrust faults pushed the rock and made small folds; later came much larger upright folds, the best known being the Sakahogi syncline.[3] Squeezed again and again over a very long time, the layers of chert ended up as wavy as they are today.

## It remembers a mass extinction

Three days later I came across this rock again at the Nagoya City Science Museum. The display was about the end-Permian mass extinction: the biggest in Earth's history, and not the one that killed the dinosaurs.

According to a 2021 paper by Onoue, Soda and Isozaki, it happened about 250 million years ago (251.94 million years), and roughly 80 per cent of marine invertebrate species disappeared.[8] Chert in the Mino Belt is normally red, but the layer from that time is black. The colour has to do with the iron in it, so scientists think the deep sea may have been short of oxygen then.[8]

> **To be honest:** the section Onoue and colleagues studied is at Iwaidani, elsewhere in the Mino Belt, not under Inuyama Castle, and the piece I touched by the river is not necessarily the extinction layer. What I can say is that the same kind of rock, in the same Mino Belt, recorded that extinction.

## What this has to do with Malaysia

![A photo I took on fieldwork in Perlis–Kedah, Malaysia, in 2022: these layers are bent in the same way](/uploads/blog/inuyama-malaysia-folds.jpg)

In the video I showed a photo from my fieldwork in Perlis–Kedah, Malaysia, in 2022. I'm only comparing the shape of the folds: in both places the layers have been squeezed into curves. I'm not saying they are the same rock or the same age.

I'd seen folding on fieldwork in Malaysia before, but this was the first time I'd seen it so clearly in Japan.

## Before you go

First, after the castle, walk down to the Kiso River; the area around Inuyama Bridge and the railway bridge is the easiest place to see the rocks.

Second, if you buy the phone version of the Inuyama Jōkamachi ticket, you can only get in and out at Meitetsu Nagoya and Inuyama stations, so don't ride past your stop.

Third, touch the rock and feel the edges of the layers, but don't hammer it or take any away.

Fourth, while you're looking at it, remember that a dozen or so layers already add up to hundreds of thousands of years.
