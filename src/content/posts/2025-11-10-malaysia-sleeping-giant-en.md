After the Segamat earthquakes, a lot of people asked themselves a question for the first time: how safe is Malaysia, really?

Most of us grew up hearing that Malaysia isn't on the Ring of Fire, so we don't get earthquakes. The first half of that is right. The second half isn't quite. In this post I want to show you what's going on under the Malay Peninsula: which plate we're on, where our earthquakes come from, and the eight faults that are thought to be potentially active.

## Where we stand

The Malay Peninsula sits on **Sundaland**, a fairly stable block of continental crust at the southern end of the Eurasian Plate. It takes in the peninsula, Sumatra, Java, Borneo and the shallow seas in between.

Stable doesn't mean still, though. To our west is one of the most active plate boundaries on the planet, the **Sumatra Subduction Zone**, where the Indian-Australian Plate slides obliquely under Sundaland at roughly 7 cm a year. The subduction zone is about 500–600 km from the nearest coast of the peninsula. Running along Sumatra itself is the **Sumatran Fault**, a long strike-slip fault about 250–300 km away from us.[1]

> Malaysia is like a sturdy house with the neighbours permanently renovating next door.

![M5+ earthquakes from 1976 to 2025, coloured by depth. Almost all of them line up along the Sumatran subduction zone, and the Malay Peninsula itself is nearly empty. Data: USGS ComCat; map: Natural Earth](/uploads/blog/malaysia-seismicity-en.png)

Two things jump out of this map. First, most earthquakes form a line off Sumatra: that's the subduction zone. Second, the further north-east you go, towards the peninsula, the deeper they get, from gold to orange-red. That's the subducting plate sinking deeper as it goes east.

So the earthquakes that affect the peninsula come from two places: big quakes far away, and the faults right under our feet.

## Source one: the giant next door

On 26 December 2004 an M9.1 earthquake struck off northern Sumatra (different studies put it between 9.1 and 9.3), one of the largest ever recorded by instruments.[2]

In Malaysia the effect everyone remembers is the tsunami, which killed 68 people in Penang, Langkawi and Kedah.[3] Fewer people know that the earthquake **physically moved the ground across Southeast Asia**. GPS stations more than 3,000 km from the epicentre recorded clear jumps, and the closer stations moved 10 cm or more.[4]

By the time seismic waves from a big Sumatran earthquake have travelled several hundred kilometres to the peninsula, they've lost much of their energy. The long-period surface waves still get through, though, and people on the west coast feel them, especially those living in high-rise buildings.[1]

> **Did you know?** Why do people in tall buildings feel distant earthquakes more? Waves from far away arrive as slow, long-period shaking, and tall buildings naturally sway at a slow rhythm too. When the two rhythms are close, the building sways noticeably. That's how someone on the 30th floor can feel dizzy while people at street level feel nothing.

## Source two: old faults, woken up

The peninsula is crossed by old faults, most of them running north-west to south-east. For a long time they were thought to be asleep.

Then, after 2004, Sumatra had a string of major earthquakes: Nias in 2005 (M8.6), Bengkulu in 2007 (M8.4) and southern Sumatra in 2009 (M7.6). Researchers believe this sequence changed the stresses across the region and reactivated some of the old faults in the peninsula.[1]

The clearest case is **Bukit Tinggi**, on the Selangor–Pahang border. From 30 November 2007 small earthquakes started coming one after another: 9 in 2007, 8 in 2008 and 5 in 2009, about 22 in total, at roughly ML 2.5–3.5.[1] Before that, the national seismic network, which MetMalaysia had been running since 2003, hadn't recorded any significant tectonic earthquake originating within the peninsula itself.

### Eight potentially active faults

The Malaysian geologist H. D. Tjia listed eight large strike-slip faults in West Malaysia that may still be active:[1]

| # | Fault zone | States |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bok Bak Fault Zone | Kedah, Perak |
| 2 | Kelau–Karak Fault Zone | Pahang |
| 3 | Lebir Fault Zone | Kelantan, Terengganu, Pahang |
| 4 | Bukit Tinggi Fault Zone | Selangor, Pahang |
| 5 | Kuala Lumpur Fault Zone | Kuala Lumpur, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan |
| 6 | Mersing Fault Zone | Johor |
| 7 | Ma Okil Fault Zone | Johor |
| 8 | Lepar Fault Zone | Pahang |

Of these, the **Bukit Tinggi** and **Kuala Lumpur** fault zones are considered the main and most active ones in West Malaysia.[1]

> **What's a strike-slip fault?** Put two blocks side by side and slide one forwards and the other backwards. They move past each other horizontally along the line where they touch. That's a strike-slip fault, quite different from a subduction zone, where one plate dives under another.

One research team looked closely at the landscape around Bukit Tinggi using radar-derived terrain maps (IFSAR) and fieldwork. They found streams that have been offset where they cross the faults and faults cutting through fairly young sediments, and concluded that several segments of the Bukit Tinggi fault zone are active. Judging from these landforms, they suggest the area has been hit by earthquakes of at least magnitude 6 in the past.[5]

That one stuck with me. Every earthquake we've actually recorded here has been small, but the landscape is telling us these faults may have done a lot more when they were awake.

## What a century of records tells us

A study of Peninsular Malaysia's earthquakes from 1922 to 2020 pulled together 59 events, with a few interesting patterns:[1]

- Magnitudes range from Mw 0.7 to 5.4, mostly in the 1s and 2s.
- Most are shallow, between 1 and 70 km deep.
- Epicentres cluster in the west and north of the peninsula and in the Strait of Malacca; the east and south are much quieter.
- The deepest was an ML 3.1 under the Strait of Malacca on 25 September 1992, 167 km down. The authors link it to a piece of slab that has broken off the Sumatran subducting plate and sunk beneath the strait.

There's also a type of earthquake that isn't caused by a fault moving on its own: **reservoir-induced earthquakes**. While the Kenyir reservoir in Terengganu was filling between 1984 and 1988, earthquakes of ML 2.4–4.6 were recorded there, and there are similar records near the Temenggor reservoir in Perak.[1] The weight of all that water, plus water seeping down and raising the pore pressure, can make nearby faults more likely to slip.

## What about Segamat?

MetMalaysia attributed the August 2025 Segamat earthquakes to the Mersing Fault Zone.[6] In the video, I overlaid the earthquake location on a map of the known faults and thought it sat some distance from all of them, including Mersing and Ma Okil. So I suggested another possibility: an undiscovered fault nearby, or a deeper structure moving.

Later, while researching my [episode on epicentres](/en/blog/epicentre-hypocentre), I found that different agencies' locations for the Segamat epicentre are more than ten kilometres apart.[7] So whether it sits on a known fault is honestly hard to say at the moment, which is exactly why the peninsula needs a denser seismometer network.

> **Further reading:** how the Segamat earthquake might have been triggered is in [my post on the Segamat earthquakes](/en/blog/segamat-earthquake).

## So, will the giant wake up?

Overall, Peninsular Malaysia is still a low-seismicity region. Every earthquake recorded so far has been modest, and none has caused deaths.

But low isn't zero. There's good evidence that distant megaquakes have reawakened old faults here, and the landscape suggests those faults have done more in the past. Knowing this isn't about scaring anyone. It's about making sure the risk is on the table when we design buildings, plan cities and prepare for emergencies.
