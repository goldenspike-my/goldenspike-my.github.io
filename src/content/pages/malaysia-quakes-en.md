## Where our earthquakes come from

The Malay Peninsula sits on Sundaland, a fairly stable block of continental crust at the southern end of the Eurasian Plate. Stable doesn't mean still, though.

To the west is the Sumatra Subduction Zone, where the Indian-Australian Plate slides obliquely under us at roughly 7 cm a year, about 500–600 km from the peninsula's nearest coast. It's the long band of dots along Sumatra on the map above. They turn from gold to orange and red towards the north-east, meaning deeper, because the sinking plate goes deeper as it heads east.

So two kinds of earthquake affect West Malaysia: big ones far away, and old faults right under our feet. By the time waves from a big Sumatran quake reach the peninsula they've lost a lot of energy, but the slow, long-period waves still get through, and people in tall buildings feel them most.

## Eight faults that may still be active

The Malaysian geologist H. D. Tjia listed eight large strike-slip faults in West Malaysia that may still be active:

| | Fault zone | States |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bok Bak Fault Zone | Kedah, Perak |
| 2 | Kelau–Karak Fault Zone | Pahang |
| 3 | Lebir Fault Zone | Kelantan, Terengganu, Pahang |
| 4 | Bukit Tinggi Fault Zone | Selangor, Pahang |
| 5 | Kuala Lumpur Fault Zone | Kuala Lumpur, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan |
| 6 | Mersing Fault Zone | Johor |
| 7 | Ma Okil Fault Zone | Johor |
| 8 | Lepar Fault Zone | Pahang |

The Bukit Tinggi and Kuala Lumpur fault zones are considered the main and most active ones. Every quake recorded in the peninsula so far has been small, but the landscape around Bukit Tinggi suggests it has had earthquakes of at least magnitude 6 in the past.

## If you feel one

Protect yourself first: drop, take cover under a table and hold on. Wait for the shaking to stop before leaving the building, and don't use the lift.

Afterwards, check MetMalaysia's website or official social media accounts. They post the epicentre, the magnitude and where it was felt. The USGS data on this page is best for larger quakes; for small local ones in the peninsula, MetMalaysia is the place to look.
