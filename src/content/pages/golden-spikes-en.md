## What a golden spike is

Geologists divide Earth's 4.6 billion years into chunks like the Jurassic and the Cretaceous. But to say where one chunk *starts*, everyone needs to agree on a reference.

A golden spike is that reference. On one real section of rock, geologists point to a single layer and say: below this is the older age, above it the younger one. Usually the signal is the first appearance of a particular fossil, or a clear chemical or magnetic change. Once the ICS has reviewed it and the IUGS has ratified it, the spike is officially in. That's where my channel gets its name.

## How to read the map

Each dot is a ratified golden spike. The colour inside it is the colour its stage has on the International Chronostratigraphic Chart, so dots from the same period look alike. Open one to see its stage, roughly how many million years ago it began, the exact layer it sits on, and the paper that ratified it.

The timeline below lets you look at one period at a time. Try the Permian: five of its eight spikes are in China and Texas, and the other three are around the Ural Mountains.

## Why so many in Europe?

Many stage names come from European places, because stratigraphy grew up in 19th-century Europe. The name and the spike don't have to be in the same place, though. The Maastrichtian is named after Maastricht in the Netherlands, but its golden spike is in a quarry in south-west France.

Italy has 13, more than any other country, and 10 of them are from the Cenozoic, mostly in seafloor sediments that were lifted up into the Apennines and Sicily. China has 11, almost all from the Palaeozoic, including Meishan in Zhejiang, where the line for the end-Permian mass extinction is drawn.

## Is there one in Malaysia?

Not yet. The nearest to Kuala Lumpur is at Penglaitan in Guangxi, China, about 2,400 km away. It defines the Wuchiapingian Stage of the Permian.

We do have very old rocks, though. The Machinchang Formation on Langkawi is Cambrian sandstone and is thought to be the oldest rock in Malaysia. It's marked on the map with a cyan ring (the position is approximate). In 2007 Langkawi also became the first UNESCO Global Geopark in Southeast Asia.

## The ones still missing

Not every boundary has its spike yet. Close to 20 stages are still waiting. Some candidate sites have been argued over for decades, and some proposals get turned down. The best-known is the Anthropocene: in March 2024 the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted against making it a formal geological epoch.

So this map will keep changing. Whenever a new spike is ratified, I'll add it.

> **About the data**: the map uses the ICS GSSP table (compiled November 2026) and only includes spikes that are ratified and have an exact location. I made a few fixes along the way. The Valanginian (ratified 2024) and Wuliuan (ratified 2018) are missing from the ICS web table, so I added them from their ratification papers. The longitudes for the Aeronian and Dapingian have typos in the table and are corrected here. And the Ediacaran golden spike was ratified in 2004.
